A waterfront property is a home and a relationship with a body of water. The second part is what makes the purchase different, and what deserves the most careful attention.

## The shoreline is part of the property

What does the frontage look like: sandy, rocky, steep, or soft? What is the condition of any seawall, dock, or stairs? Is the shoreline stable? Photographs rarely answer these questions, so look closely and bring the right experts.

## Access and rights vary

Private frontage, shared beach rights, and easement access are not the same. Confirm what actually conveys with the property, in writing, before you rely on it.

## Orientation, privacy, and use

Consider which way the home faces, how visible it is from the water and from neighbors, and how you intend to use it. A seasonal retreat and a year-round home place different demands on a property.

## Maintenance is part of the cost

Water, ice, wind, and moisture are tough on structures. Docks, walls, decks, and drainage need ongoing care. Ask about the history of maintenance and repairs, and think realistically about the upkeep you are taking on.

## Regulations belong with the experts

Rules affecting shorelines, docks, wetlands, floodplains, and water use can be specific to a property and can change. Those questions belong with the appropriate governmental authority or a qualified professional. State agencies such as EGLE are a starting point for understanding which programs may apply.

## Think about resale

The features that make a waterfront property special to you may or may not be those future buyers value most. It helps to consider both as you decide.

## Due diligence deserves extra time

Plan for inspections and investigations beyond the standard ones. Build that time into your timeline, and decide in advance which findings would change your decision.
